Glicko FAQ

So what is this number next to your name, and why does it keep changing? This page explains where the rating comes from, what it actually means, and how Glicko-2 is adapted for RB48.

If you want to dive deeper into the underlying system, the German Wikipedia article on the Glicko system provides a more technical overview of Glicko and Glicko-2.

What is a Glicko rating?

A Glicko rating is a mathematical way of summarising the results of previous matches and using them to estimate how likely a player is to win future matches.

It is important to be precise about what that means: the rating is a result-based performance measure, not a measurement of a player's actual or intrinsic strength.

If someone has a high rating, it means that the results associated with that rating have been strong relative to the opponents and teammates involved. It does not mean that the number has discovered some objective measure of how good that person "really" is.

Where does Glicko come from?

Glicko is part of a longer line of rating systems, with its roots going back to the Elo rating system.

Elo was originally developed for chess. The basic idea is surprisingly simple: instead of merely saying that one player is better than another, a rating can be used to calculate the probability of a particular result.

If two players have similar ratings, a roughly even result is expected. If one player has a substantially higher rating, that player is expected to win more often.

After an actual match, the result can then be compared with what was expected. A player who performs better than expected gains rating; a player who performs worse than expected loses rating.

So is Elo basically a win-probability system?

Yes. That's one of the most important ideas behind the whole family of rating systems.

A rating difference can be converted into an expected score. The larger the difference between two ratings, the more strongly the system expects one side to win.

This creates a useful feedback loop:

  1. Ratings produce an expected result.
  2. The match produces an actual result.
  3. The difference between expected and actual performance changes the ratings.
  4. The new ratings produce new expectations for future matches.

Glicko keeps this basic idea, but adds information about how uncertain a rating currently is.

So what does Glicko-2 add?

What is RD?

RD stands for Rating Deviation.

Think of it as an uncertainty indicator. A rating of 1600 with a low RD represents a much more established estimate than a rating of 1600 with a high RD.

This matters because the system should not treat ten matches of evidence in exactly the same way as a single match.

A player with very little recent information has a less certain rating. As more results accumulate, the system can become more confident.

Why does RD matter when looking at the stats page?

Imagine two players:

  • Player A: rating 1650, RD 20
  • Player B: rating 1700, RD 100

Player B has the higher raw rating, but there is considerably more uncertainty around it.

For the ordering on the stats page, RB48 therefore uses a conservative rating:

Conservative Rating = Rating − 3 × RD

This favours ratings that are both high and well established. It is deliberately conservative: uncertainty is treated as a reason not to assume that the player's current rating is fully representative.

Glicko 2 contains some other variables not present in the statspage, check the Wikipedia article for more details.

What exactly is the RB48 rating measuring?

Is a 1600-rated player "better" than a 1400-rated player?

Not in any absolute sense.

The rating is based on match results. It describes how a player's observed results have translated into the rating model's expectations.

Football contains a huge number of things that a rating like this does not directly measure: fitness, injuries, tactics, positioning, communication, passing quality, finishing, defending, chemistry, playing style, and simply what happened to occur during a particular match.

None of those things needs to be converted into a number for the system to work.

Consequently, a rating should not be treated as a verdict on a player. It is better understood as: "What does the match history currently imply according to this particular model?"

Why not just use win%?

Win% tells us how often a player won, but not how difficult those wins were.

For example, imagine two players:

  • Player A wins 60% of their matches, but frequently beats teams rated much higher than their own.
  • Player B wins 75% of their matches, but mostly had expected wins.

A simple win% would rate Player B higher. Glicko can rate Player A higher because beating a strong opponent is a more impressive result than an expected win. The rating therefore considers not only the result itself, but also how that result compares to what was expected.

Why are there three ratings?

What are TOTAL, BOX and HF?

RB48 keeps three separate rating streams:

  • TOTAL — the overall rating across all relevant matches.
  • BOX — the rating based on Box matches.
  • HF — the rating based on Half-Field matches.

These are independent rating streams. A result in a Box match changes the Box rating and the Total rating, while the Half-Field rating is kept separate. Likewise, HF only affects HF and Total, but not BOX games. The TOTAL rating stream takes all games into account.

This allows the stats page to show how the results behave in the different formats.

How does a team rating work?

Football is a team game, so how can an individual rating change?

This is one of the unavoidable compromises of applying an individual rating system to team football.

A match gives us a team result, not an isolated result for every individual player. RB48 therefore uses the ratings of the players on each team to calculate a team rating.

The team ratings determine the expected result. The actual team result is then compared with that expectation, and the individual players receive rating updates based on the match.

In simplified terms:

players → team ratings → expected result → actual result → rating updates

This means that a player does not receive a rating update because the system knows exactly how well they personally played. Instead, they receive an update because they participated in a match whose result contained information relevant to the rating model.

The individual rating change of each player on a team can differ based on their personal RD at the time of the match. For each player, the update therefore considers:

  • Player Team Rating
  • (Personal RD)*
  • Opponent Team Rating
  • Opponent Team RD

Player Team Rating is calculated as the arithmetic mean of the ratings of the players on the team.

Team RD is calculated as the quadratic mean of the players' RDs. This gives larger RD values more influence, reflecting the increased uncertainty they introduce to the team rating.

(Personal RD)* combines the player's individual uncertainty with the uncertainty of their team:

(Personal RD)* = (Personal RD + Team RD) / 2

This means that the rating update reflects both the uncertainty of the individual player's rating and the uncertainty of the team as a whole.

How does the rating translate into a probability?

The difference between two team ratings determines how strongly the model favours one team over the other. The expected score can be calculated precisely using the Glicko-2 formula:

E = 1 / (1 + 10-g(RD) · (R1 - R2) / 400)

The function g(RD) accounts for the uncertainty of the ratings:

g(RD) = 1 / √(1 + 3q²RD² / π²)
where q = ln(10) / 400.

In other words, the expected result depends not only on the difference between the ratings, but also on how certain those ratings are.

For example:

  • Team A: 1696 rating, RD 148
  • Team B: 1480 rating, RD 148

The Glicko-2 model gives Team A an expected score of approximately 75.5%, leaving Team B with approximately 24.5%.

Now consider:

  • Team A: 1480 rating, RD 48
  • Team B: 1700 rating, RD 148

Team B is again strongly favoured, with an expected score of approximately 75.9%.

As RB48's database grows, we will test how accurately these model probabilities predict actual match results. That gives us a way to evaluate the model empirically rather than simply assuming that the mathematical model must be accurate.

Does winning against a stronger team give more rating?

Generally, yes.

A result that was already strongly expected contains less new information than a result that was unlikely according to the current ratings.

Beating a team that the model expected to beat you is therefore more informative than beating a team that was already heavily favoured.

What happens when someone doesn't play?

Does my rating decrease if I don't play?

No. Missing a match does not directly lower the rating itself.

However, the system becomes less certain about an old rating as time passes without new evidence. Glicko represents this by increasing RD.

RB48 implements this through a match-based inactivity rule rather than simply using calendar time.

How does inactivity work in RB48?

RD increases linearly for missed matches.

The three rating streams have slightly different inactivity rules:

  • TOTAL RD increases for every missed match, regardless of whether it was Box or Half-Field.
  • BOX RD increases when a player misses a Box match.
  • HF RD increases when a player misses a Half-Field match.

This means that inactivity increases uncertainty rather than simply punishing someone with lost rating points.

What happens when a new player joins?

Why doesn't everyone start with a completely arbitrary rating?

New players have very little information available, so their initial rating is necessarily an assumption.

RB48 starts new ratings from the standard Glicko-2 starting point and deliberately gives new ratings a relatively large RD.

In other words, the system starts with the attitude: "We have very little evidence about this player yet."

Actual match results can then move the rating and reduce the uncertainty as more information becomes available.

How does RB48 handle calibration?

New ratings begin with a relatively large RD because there is little evidence about a new player. This allows the first few results to have an appropriately large influence on the rating while the system is still uncertain.

In exceptional cases, RB48 can also set calibration values manually. This is intended only for extreme performance outliers where starting from the ordinary initial rating would create an unnecessarily large disturbance in the rating system.

No manual calibration is used for regular players. The normal process is simply to start with the standard initial values and let the match results establish the rating.

What about external players?

Why do some matches show an "External Player"?

Not every person appearing in a match is necessarily part of the RB48 player pool.

When an alias is marked as ignored, RB48 does not create or maintain a normal player rating for that person. Instead, their participation can still be represented in the match as an External Player. The external player contributes a larger amount of uncertainty to the Team's RD value.

This means the historical match information remains complete without introducing an unrated person into the normal player statistics.

Why do ratings sometimes change by a surprisingly large amount?

A rating change is not simply a fixed number of points for a win or loss. It depends on the circumstances of the match.

Among other things, the calculation considers:

  • the player's current rating,
  • the player's RD,
  • the ratings and uncertainty of the opposing players,
  • the expected result,
  • and the actual result.

Consequently, the same win can produce different rating changes at different points in a player's rating history.

So what should I actually take away from my rating?

Probably this:

Your rating is a statistical summary of your match results, interpreted through the Glicko-2 model.

It is useful for comparing historical results, estimating expected outcomes, tracking how those estimates change over time, and creating balanced teams.

It is not a judgement, a skill certificate, or a measure of personal worth.

The number is there because statistics are useful. You do not need to take it personally.
